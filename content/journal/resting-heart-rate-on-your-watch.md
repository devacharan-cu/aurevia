Many patients now arrive with a year of heart-rate data on their wrist. It can be genuinely useful. It can also generate a surprising amount of worry, most of it unnecessary.

## What a normal resting rate looks like

For adults, a resting heart rate between 50 and 100 beats a minute is conventionally considered normal, and many fit people sit comfortably below 60. The absolute number matters less than two things: whether it is changing, and whether it comes with symptoms.

## Patterns worth noticing

- **A sustained rise in your usual resting rate** over several weeks, without an obvious explanation such as illness, poor sleep, alcohol or a new medicine.
- **Episodes of a very fast rate at rest**, particularly if they start and stop abruptly.
- **An irregular-rhythm notification**, especially if it recurs. A notification is not a diagnosis, but it deserves a proper ECG.
- **A low rate with symptoms** such as dizziness, near-fainting or breathlessness, rather than a low rate on its own.

## Patterns that usually aren't a concern

Heart rate varies a great deal through an ordinary day. It rises after coffee, a difficult meeting, a large meal or a glass of wine, and it is often lower overnight than people expect; rates in the forties during sleep are common in healthy adults. Brief, single "skipped beats" are extremely common and usually harmless, particularly when they are most noticeable at rest and settle with exercise.

## When not to wait

Chest pain, fainting, severe breathlessness, or palpitations accompanied by any of these need urgent assessment. Call 999 rather than booking an appointment. A watch is not an emergency device, and a normal reading should never delay seeking help.

## What happens in clinic

When someone brings their data, I look at it; it often helps. Then I take a history, because the circumstances of an episode usually tell me more than the tracing. A 12-lead ECG takes a few minutes. If symptoms come and go, we fit a small monitor for anywhere from 24 hours to seven days, long enough to catch the event itself. An echocardiogram shows the structure of the heart if there is any reason to question it.

The most common outcome is reassurance with an explanation. The next most common is a clear, treatable finding. Both are better than months of watching a number.
