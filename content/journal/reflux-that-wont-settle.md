Heartburn and acid reflux are extremely common, and most episodes are managed perfectly well with simple measures and, when needed, a short course of medication. This note is for the reflux that doesn't behave: symptoms that return every time treatment stops, or never quite go away.

## First, the symptoms that shouldn't wait

See a doctor promptly, rather than in a few months, if reflux or indigestion comes with any of the following:

- Difficulty swallowing, or food sticking
- Weight loss you can't explain
- Persistent vomiting
- Vomiting blood, or black, tarry stools
- New, persistent symptoms after the age of 55

These don't usually mean anything serious, but they are the features that warrant an endoscopy without delay.

## The checklist

**Is it actually reflux?** Burning behind the breastbone after meals or when lying down is typical. But chest discomfort has other causes, including the heart, and some people with reflux have mostly throat symptoms: a hoarse voice, a lingering cough, or a sense of something caught in the throat.

**How is the medicine being taken?** Proton pump inhibitors such as omeprazole and lansoprazole work best taken 30 to 60 minutes before a meal, usually breakfast. Taken at bedtime or with food they are considerably less effective. This one change helps a surprising number of people.

**What happens in the evening?** Large, late meals and lying down soon afterwards are among the commonest triggers. Leaving about three hours between the last meal and bed, and raising the head of the bed rather than adding pillows, both help.

**What else is on the list?** Alcohol, smoking and certain medicines, including anti-inflammatory painkillers, can make reflux worse. Weight carried around the abdomen increases pressure on the stomach.

**Has it been investigated?** For persistent symptoms, an endoscopy can look for inflammation, a hiatus hernia, and changes in the lining of the gullet that benefit from surveillance. Some people also benefit from tests that measure acid exposure directly over a day or more.

## Long-term medicine

Many people take acid-suppressing medicine for years. For some that is entirely appropriate. For others it is worth stepping down to the lowest dose that controls symptoms, or taking it only when needed. That is a decision best made with a clinician rather than by stopping abruptly, which can cause a temporary rebound in symptoms.
