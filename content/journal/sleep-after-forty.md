Sleep changes with age, and not only because of stress. From midlife, most people spend less time in deep sleep, wake more often during the night, and find that sleepiness arrives earlier in the evening. None of this means something is wrong. It does mean that habits which worked at thirty may stop working at fifty.

## Causes worth ruling out first

Before treating insomnia, it is worth looking for something that is driving it:

- **Sleep apnoea**: loud snoring, pauses in breathing noticed by a partner, and daytime sleepiness. It is under-diagnosed, particularly in women.
- **Perimenopause and menopause**, through night sweats and hormonal effects on sleep itself.
- **Waking to pass urine**, which has its own causes worth addressing.
- **Low mood and anxiety**, which both disturb sleep and are worsened by it.
- **Alcohol**, which helps some people fall asleep but reliably fragments the second half of the night.
- **Medicines**, including some antidepressants, beta-blockers and steroids.

## What actually helps

The most effective treatment for long-term insomnia is cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia, known as CBT-I. It is a structured programme, usually over about six weeks, and it is recommended in clinical guidelines as the first treatment to try.

CBT-I works on the habits and thoughts that keep insomnia going, and some of it is counter-intuitive. It often involves spending *less* time in bed at first, so that the time you do spend there is consolidated sleep rather than frustrated wakefulness. Most people notice a difference within a few weeks and, unlike sleeping tablets, the benefit tends to last.

## Small things worth doing

- Keep a consistent wake time, weekends included. It anchors everything else.
- Get daylight in the morning, outside if you can.
- If you are awake and frustrated for what feels like twenty minutes or more, get up, do something quiet in dim light, and return when sleepy.
- Keep caffeine to the morning, and notice what alcohol does to your night.
- Turn the clock away from the bed.

## Things that tend not to help

Lying in bed trying harder. Long daytime naps. Most over-the-counter sleep aids. And, for some people, sleep trackers, which can turn every night into an examination.

If poor sleep has lasted more than three months, is affecting your days, or comes with loud snoring or pauses in breathing, it is worth a proper assessment.
