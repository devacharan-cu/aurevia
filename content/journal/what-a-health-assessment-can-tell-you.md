A health assessment is an unusual sort of appointment. Most medical visits begin with a problem. This one usually begins without one, and that changes what the tests can honestly do.

## What it does well

It is good at finding things that are common, silent and treatable. Raised blood pressure is the clearest example: it rarely causes symptoms, it is easy to measure badly, and treating it meaningfully lowers the risk of stroke and heart attack. The same is true of raised cholesterol in someone whose overall risk is high, of type 2 diabetes and the prediabetes that precedes it, and of some cancers, where screening at the right age finds them earlier.

It is also good at establishing a baseline. A kidney function result, a weight, an ECG, a grip-strength reading: none may matter much today, but a change over five years can matter a great deal, and you can only see a change if you measured the first time.

## What it does less well

It is not good at predicting the future of one particular person. Risk scores are calculated from large populations. A ten per cent ten-year risk of heart disease means that, of a hundred people like you, about ten would have an event over a decade. It does not say which ten.

Nor is it good at ruling things out completely. A normal resting ECG does not exclude a rhythm problem that appears only on exertion or at night. A normal blood count does not mean every serious illness is absent. We would rather say that plainly than let a clean report become false reassurance.

## Why we don't do every test

More tests can feel like more thoroughness. Often they aren't. Every test has a false-positive rate, and when many tests are run in someone with no symptoms, the chance that at least one comes back "abnormal" by chance alone rises quickly. Each of those findings needs following up, sometimes with further imaging or procedures that carry their own small risks.

Whole-body scans in people without symptoms are the obvious example. They commonly find incidental nodules and cysts, most of them harmless, which can take months of repeat imaging to settle. We will recommend a specific scan when your history points to it. We won't sell one as a default.

## What we look for instead

The most informative part of an assessment is usually the hour of conversation at the start: what your parents and siblings developed and at what age, what you drink and how you sleep, what has changed in the last year that you haven't mentioned to anyone. That history decides which tests are worth doing.

The results are then only half of what you leave with. The other half is a short list, usually three things and rarely more than five, of what is actually worth doing about them.

> A good assessment should leave you with fewer worries than you arrived with, and a clearer view of the ones that remain.

## How often

For most healthy adults under forty, a thorough assessment every three to five years is reasonable, with blood pressure checked more often. Over forty, or with a significant family history, many people choose an annual review. The right interval depends on what was found last time, and it is a fair question to ask your physician directly.
