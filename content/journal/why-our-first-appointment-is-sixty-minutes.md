When we opened, the length of the first appointment was the decision we argued over longest. Sixty minutes is expensive to offer and harder to schedule. We chose it anyway, and the reasons are more practical than philosophical.

## Most of a diagnosis is in the history

It is an old teaching in medicine that the history, the story of what is happening, told and questioned properly, provides most of the information needed for a diagnosis. Examination and tests confirm or refine it. In a short appointment, the history is what gets squeezed: questions become closed, and the second or third concern never comes up.

## People rarely lead with the real reason

A familiar pattern in general practice is that the most important concern is raised last, sometimes with a hand already on the door. It isn't evasiveness. It takes time to feel comfortable, and it is natural to start with the easier problem. An hour leaves room for the conversation to reach the thing that matters.

## Fewer tests, not more

It might seem that a longer appointment would lead to more investigations. In our experience the opposite is often true. When there is time to understand a symptom properly, it is easier to be confident about what *doesn't* need testing, and to explain why.

## Where the hour goes

Roughly twenty-five minutes of history: family history, medicines, work, sleep, and what you would like from the visit. Ten to fifteen minutes of examination, where it is needed. The rest is explanation and a plan made together, with time for your questions, including the ones you thought of on the way in.

> The first hour isn't a luxury repeated at every visit. It is what makes every later appointment shorter.

## After the first appointment

Follow-up appointments are usually thirty minutes, sometimes less. Because your physician already knows the story, those shorter visits can go straight to what has changed.
